Matching the material to the surface

The trick to a bathroom that lasts is using the right material in the right place. Here's how the surfaces differ.

Shower wallsPorcelain and glass are ideal — near-zero water absorption, easy to keep clean. Large-format panels cut grout lines to a minimum for a spa-like wall.
Shower & bath floorsSmaller mosaics win here: the extra grout adds grip and follows the slope to the drain. Choose textured or matte finishes for wet-floor safety.
Natural stoneMarble, travertine, and limestone bring warmth. Porous, so seal on install and favor honed finishes to hide etching and spots.
Accents & nichesGlass and mosaic strips, shelves, and shower niches add the detail that makes a bathroom feel custom without a full-room splurge.

One more thing worth planning early: waterproofing behind the tile matters as much as the tile itself. We're glad to talk through substrate and setting materials with you or your installer so the finished surface actually stays watertight.

Bathroom tile FAQ

What tile works best in a shower?
Porcelain and glass tile are the most reliable shower surfaces: they absorb almost no water and shrug off soap and humidity. On shower floors, smaller mosaics are the smart pick — the extra grout lines add grip and let the tile follow the slope to the drain. Natural stone can be stunning in a shower but is porous, so it needs sealing and ongoing care. Waterproofing behind the tile matters as much as the tile itself, so plan the substrate with your installer.
Is natural stone a good idea in a bathroom?
Yes, with the right expectations. Marble, travertine, and limestone bring warmth and character no manufactured tile fully copies. Because stone is porous, it should be sealed on installation and resealed periodically, and honed finishes hide etching and water spots better than polished ones in a wet room. If you want the stone look with near-zero upkeep, stone-look porcelain is an excellent stand-in and we stock both.
What tile is safe for a bathroom floor?
Look for floor-rated tile with enough slip resistance for a wet room — textured or matte porcelain, or mosaics whose grout lines add traction. Large, highly polished tiles look great but can be slick when wet, so they're better on walls than on a shower or bathroom floor. Tell us the room and we'll steer you to floor-appropriate options.
